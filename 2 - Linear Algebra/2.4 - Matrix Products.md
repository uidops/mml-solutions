# Exercise 2.4 — Matrix Products

> [!question] Problem
> **2.4** Compute the following matrix products, if possible:
>
> a.
>
> $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 5 \\ 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
>
> b.
>
> $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
>
> c.
>
> $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix}$$
>
> d.
>
> $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 1 & -1 & -4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$
>
> e.
>
> $$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 1 & -1 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$$

## Solution

> [!error] (a) — the product is not defined
> The first matrix is $3 \times 2$ and the second is $3 \times 3$. For a matrix product the number of columns of the first factor must equal the number of rows of the second, but here $2 \neq 3$. Therefore this product cannot be computed. $\blacksquare$
>

> [!success] (b) — a $3 \times 3$ product
> By the matrix product rule (each entry is a row of the first factor times a column of the second),
>
> $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 & 5 \\ 10 & 9 & 11 \\ 16 & 15 & 17 \end{bmatrix}.$$
>
> For example, the $(1,1)$ entry is $1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 0 + 3 \cdot 1 = 4$ and the $(2,3)$ entry is $4 \cdot 0 + 5 \cdot 1 + 6 \cdot 1 = 11$. $\blacksquare$
>

> [!success] (c) — a $3 \times 3$ product
> With the factors in the opposite order,
>
> $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 7 & 9 \\ 11 & 13 & 15 \\ 8 & 10 & 12 \end{bmatrix}.$$
>
> For example, the $(1,1)$ entry is $1 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 4 + 0 \cdot 7 = 5$ and the $(2,3)$ entry is $0 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 6 + 1 \cdot 9 = 15$. Comparing with (b), the products in different orders differ — matrix multiplication is not commutative. $\blacksquare$
>

> [!success] (d) — a $2 \times 2$ product
> A $2 \times 4$ matrix times a $4 \times 2$ matrix has shape $2 \times 2$:
>
> $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 1 & -1 & -4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 14 & 6 \\ -21 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.$$
>
> For example, the $(1,2)$ entry is $1 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 = 6$ and the $(2,1)$ entry is $4 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 + (-1) \cdot 2 + (-4) \cdot 5 = -21$. $\blacksquare$
>

> [!success] (e) — a $4 \times 4$ product
> A $4 \times 2$ matrix times a $2 \times 4$ matrix has shape $4 \times 4$:
>
> $$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 4 & 1 & -1 & -4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 & 3 & -3 & -12 \\ -3 & 1 & 2 & 6 \\ 6 & 5 & 1 & 0 \\ 13 & 12 & 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.$$
>
> For example, the $(1,3)$ entry is $0 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot (-1) = -3$ and the $(4,3)$ entry is $5 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot (-1) = 3$. Note that (d) and (e) multiply the same two matrices in opposite orders and produce different shapes and values. $\blacksquare$
